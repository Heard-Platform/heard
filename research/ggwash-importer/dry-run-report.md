This week, Lime began rolling out 1,000 of its newest shared e-bikes across the District.

The discussion is no longer limited to whether these transportation options are available or efficient. Increasingly, it is about whether people feel able, willing, and comfortable enough to use them.
We’ve asked our Lime riders across the District, and they are telling us the same thing.
One rider recently described using a shared e-bike to get to a volunteer event near the National Mall. Another told us that Lime “allowed me to travel with a broken foot.” Others described using Lime for doctor’s appointments, grocery trips, errands, work, and connections to the train. Their experiences show how riders rely on shared micromobility for very different reasons and at very different moments in their lives.
These riders were not looking for a new hobby. They were looking for practical ways to move around the District.
That distinction matters because shared bikes and scooters are often most useful in the spaces between other transportation options. They help connect people to transit, shorten first- and last-mile trips, and make multimodal travel more convenient when a trip does not fit neatly into a single mode of transportation.
For many riders, the value of an e-bike is that it can make everyday trips feel more manageable.
“One day, I rented a Lime bike when walking across the city became too exhausting, and it completely changed how I moved through my day. The electric assist helped me get to work, run errands, and meet friends without worrying about pain or fatigue slowing me down. For the first time in a while, I felt independent because I could travel farther and enjoy the city on my own terms.”
— Zach Z.
Many people who could benefit from an e-bike do not think of themselves as cyclists. Some have not ridden in years. Others are intimidated by traditional bike designs. Many simply want a vehicle that feels stable, intuitive, and easy to use from the moment they step on.
That feedback shapes how new vehicles are designed.
That feedback increasingly shapes how new vehicles are designed.

Washington is one of the greatest cities in the world. It is also becoming one of the most expensive places to live in America. And we are running out of time to fix it.
Average rents in the District now exceed $2,400 a month. Median home prices are approaching $700,000. Teachers, firefighters, nurses, young professionals, and the families who built this city are being pushed out. Not because they don’t love DC, but because DC has made it too expensive to stay.
I grew up in Stronghold in Northeast DC. I raised my family here and I represented Ward 5 on the DC Council for ten years before being elected At-Large in 2022. Ward 5 has historically absorbed more than its share of the city’s industrial uses, infrastructure burdens, and trash transfer stations. For too long, Ward 5 was told to take what the city needed and expect little in return. I know what it feels like when your neighborhood is left behind. I also know what it looks like when good housing projects get strangled before they ever break ground.
I watched McMillan sit undeveloped for nearly a decade while litigation dragged on. That delay didn’t just postpone more than 600 homes for Ward 5. It meant hundreds of families spent years longer waiting for housing they needed, while DC home prices climbed more than 60% between 2016 and today. I am not willing to let that happen again.
A record built on results
I have spent my career in public office rolling up my sleeves and creating pathways for more housing. As a councilmember, I introduced legislation establishing the industrial land transformation task force to unlock underutilized land for housing and economic opportunity. I also authored a law to guarantee affordable housing for lower-income households when private developers purchase District-owned land. I supported the revolving Affordable Housing Preservation Fund and fought year after year for budget funding for the Housing Production Trust Fund. 
Finally, when it became clear that our court system was being abused as a tool to stop economic growth in Stronghold, I stepped in (as I have in various ways with multiple other critical housing projects) to ensure Northeast saw the addition of those more than 600 new homes, including affordable and senior housing, a grocery store, community center with a pool, a six-acre park, and more than one million square feet of desperately needed health care space.

I passed legislation prohibiting housing discrimination based on criminal background or arrest history, because a fair housing market means a fair shot for everyone.
That record is the foundation of my housing platform: building more housing for all and preserving our existing stock of affordable homes. According to a recent analysis from Up For Growth, we’re part of a region that has one of the highest rates of housing underproduction in the country, but it’s also a place where it’s financially feasible to build middle-income housing – if only more supportive zoning and land use regulations were in place.
Regulations account for roughly 40% of what it costs to build multifamily housing in the US. DC has the tools our neighbors lack: a Comprehensive Plan update, zoning reform authority, and a commitment to use them. As mayor, my first priority is making Washington the easiest city in the region in which to build housing.
When it comes to building income-restricted affordable housing, it’s a similar picture, which is why preservation of existing units is so important. We are spending between $700,000 and $1 million per unit to build affordable housing in this city, more than the median DC home sells for. A typical affordable housing project in DC pieces together money from 5 to ten different sources, each with its own timeline and fees. That is a structural failure and I intend to lead us beyond reactive, one-off fixes to durable, systematic solutions.

Instead of the old PUD approach, we need to create a more predictable, less transactional incentive zoning structure that transparently motivates developers to produce quality assets that meet city needs without discretionary processes while still engaging communities – for example, through increased small area planning.
Most recently, the Office of Planning released a draft Future Land Use Map that would lock in the status quo of rising prices, exclusionary zoning, and displacement. A plan meant to carry this city to 2050 cannot be built around protecting segregation, widening the racial wealth gap, or Black residents leaving. I will send that map back to the Office of Planning with a clear directive: add high density housing capacity along major corridors, expand density designations to include more “missing middle” options to deploy citywide, and stop treating growth like a threat.

If you have ever driven, biked, walked, or waited for a bus at the intersection of Pennsylvania and Minnesota Avenue SE, you already know the problem. It is chaotic, confusing, and unsafe. The community has known this for years, and so has the District Department of Transportation, which is why the Pennsylvania Ave SE and Minnesota Ave SE Intersection Safety Improvement Project has been nearly two decades in the making.
ANC 7B wants this intersection fixed. Let me be unambiguous about that, because it seems to get lost in the back-and-forth: We are not trying to kill this project. We are trying to make sure that after 19 years of planning, what gets built actually serves the people who live here.
So far, we are not confident it will.
A process that got off on the wrong foot
The first version of DDOT’s design would have severed access from Pennsylvania Avenue to 25th Street, a route used by WMATA bus route D10 and the only street in the immediate area with a bike lane. Unprotected as it is, cutting it off would have eliminated the one available exit from this chaotic intersection onto a street where cyclists are at least nominally accounted for.
When ANC 7B formally disapproved the plan in March, DDOT began implementing it anyway — just one business day after the public comment period closed. The comment period closed on a Friday; construction began Monday morning. After we raised the alarm and engaged Ward 7 Councilmember Wendell Felder’s office, DDOT stopped, rolled back the changes, and agreed to revise the design. The updated plan keeps 25th Street open, a real win. In April, ANC 7B passed a resolution of conditional support outlining what else we needed to see: rush-hour bus lanes on Pennsylvania Avenue between Prout and 27th Streets, converting to parking during non-peak hours, and protected bike infrastructure throughout the project scope.
None of those conditions were met.
The 10-lane problem and why it’s a showstopper
When DDOT returned to the ANC in May, they didn’t send the designers. They sent the builders to tell us construction starts in June. The new design retained what has emerged as our most fundamental objection: extending the left-turn lanes from eastbound Pennsylvania Avenue to northbound Minnesota Avenue, expanding the 2300 block of Pennsylvania Avenue from eight lanes to 10. This is a nonstarter for ANC 7B, and the community has been unequivocal about it.
DDOT insisted these aren’t “new lanes” because they are turning lanes, not travel lanes. That distinction does not hold up for a pedestrian trying to cross the street. The design creates 11 points where a pedestrian can encounter a vehicle: 10 lanes, plus a right turn from L’Enfant Square feeding directly into the crosswalk. That crosswalk will be controlled by a pedestrian-only signal, the kind drivers are far more likely to ignore since there is no risk of hitting another vehicle and these signals are rarely camera-enforced.
DDOT argues the design is necessary because drivers exiting 295 currently cause crashes trying to cut across four lanes to reach the left-turn lane onto Minnesota Avenue. We understand the problem. But the 295/Pennsylvania Avenue interchange is not being modified in this project, meaning those same drivers will now need to cross five or six lanes instead of four. It is hard to see how that is safer. When I asked DDOT to name another neighborhood arterial in DC carrying 10 lanes of traffic, they could not. Ward 7 residents should not have to serve as the District’s test case to find out what happens when you put 10 lanes through a neighborhood.
A road that works against its own neighborhood
In FY2021, the DC Council funded the Pennsylvania Avenue East Small Area Plan to spark economic development along Pennsylvania Avenue from the John Philip Sousa Bridge to the Maryland border. The 2300 block, the very block at the center of this redesign, is part of the plan’s first phase.
Businesses only thrive when customers can reach them. Right now, that is a problem: Pedestrians don’t feel safe due to chaotic traffic, cyclists have no protected infrastructure, buses crawl through congestion, and there is nowhere to park to stop and shop. The community’s vision addresses all of this. DDOT’s does not, and the design makes future improvements harder, not easier. Under the current proposal, the median would be significantly reduced to accommodate two additional turning lanes — and it is precisely where protected bike infrastructure should go. Once that space is gone, so is that opportunity. The Council invested in a plan for this neighborhood’s future. The intersection redesign should be consistent with that vision, not in conflict with it.
Two issues, not one
DDOT has at times treated all of our objections as equivalent. They are not.
On bus and bike infrastructure, we accept that federal funding restrictions are real under the current administration. We are not asking DDOT to violate those rules. We are asking for a written commitment, with a timeline, for when these improvements will follow using local dollars. A promise without a deadline is not a plan — and communities east of the river have a long memory for promises that never materialize. We are always told our turn is coming; it never seems to come.
On the lane expansion, there is no federal constraint in play. That is a choice, and it is the wrong one. Residents at community association meetings have said they would rather see the funding lost entirely than accept a 10-lane road in their community. That is how strongly people feel, and it is why this remains a showstopper regardless of what else is resolved.
Our asks are straightforward: remove the lane expansion, commit in writing to a timeline for bus and bike improvements, and build something consistent with the small area plan the council already funded. That is what we are asking for, and it is not unreasonable.
A community meeting is scheduled for June 13 at 11:00 am at the DC Dream Center (2826 Q Street SE), co-hosted by ANC 7B and ANC 8A. Virtual attendance is available here. If you care about how this intersection gets built, and you should, we hope to see you there.
